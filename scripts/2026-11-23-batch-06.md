## What If You Lived on Mercury?
`b6_mercury` · 1495 words (incl. like/subscribe line) · 9:48

*[hook · scene me6_hook]*
On November twenty-first, 2026, after more than eight years in space, a European and Japanese spacecraft fired its engine, and let Mercury catch it.
It's called BepiColombo. And it's only the second mission ever to orbit the planet closest to the Sun.
So what if you'd been on board? What if you landed on Mercury, and tried to live there?

**[CARD: WHAT IF YOU LIVED ON MERCURY?]**

*[news · scene me6_news]*
First, what actually happened. The big engine burn began on the evening of November twenty-first, European time. Just after midnight, ESA's flight dynamics team confirmed it. BepiColombo had been captured into orbit.
It's really two spacecraft stacked together. ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter, and Japan's Mio, built to study the planet's magnetic field. They're due to split apart on December ninth and tenth.
Science is set to begin in April 2027. Until then, each orbiter still has to be steered into its final orbit, so what they'll discover is still unknown.
Only one spacecraft has orbited Mercury before. NASA's Messenger circled it from 2011 to 2015, then ended its mission by crashing into the surface.

**[CARD: GETTING THERE]**

*[trip · scene me6_trip]*
Here's a surprise. Mercury is hard to reach. Not because it's far away, but because the Sun is in the way.
Fall toward the Sun and you speed up, like a car rolling downhill. To be captured by little Mercury, you have to shed a huge amount of that speed.
According to some estimates, it takes less energy to reach Pluto than to reach Mercury.
So BepiColombo braked the clever way, with nine flybys. One of Earth, two of Venus, and six of Mercury itself. Each one stole a little speed.
The mission is named after Italian scientist Giuseppe Colombo, nicknamed Bepi. In 1970, he worked out how NASA's Mariner 10 could loop back and fly past Mercury a second time.

**[CARD: TOUCHDOWN]**

*[land · scene me6_land]*
Now the rules. You ride down in a lander, with a heat-proof suit and a small base. We'll ignore the cost. Your only job is to stay alive.
You touch down at dawn, right on the line between night and day. And the first thing you notice is how light you feel. Mercury's gravity is only about thirty-eight percent of Earth's. A ten kilogram backpack feels like less than four.
Then you look up. The sky is black, even in daylight. Mercury has no real atmosphere. Just a thin haze of atoms called an exosphere, knocked off the ground by the solar wind and tiny meteorites.
And the Sun is enormous. It can look more than three times wider than it does from Earth, and its light can be as much as seven times brighter.

**[CARD: THE FIRST HOUR]**

*[heat · scene me6_heat]*
In your first hour, the sunlight starts to bite. In full daylight, Mercury's ground can reach about four hundred and thirty degrees Celsius. That's hot enough to melt lead.
But step into the shadow of a boulder, and it's a different world. With no air to carry heat around, shade stays cold. On the night side, the ground drops to about minus one hundred and eighty degrees.
That's a swing of about six hundred degrees between day and night.
Luckily, you won't have to face it all at once. Because on Mercury, the Sun barely moves.

**[CARD: THE FIRST SUNRISE]**

*[dawn · scene me6_dawn]*
Mercury spins very slowly. One turn takes about fifty-nine Earth days. Meanwhile, it races around the Sun in just eighty-eight.
Put those together, and something strange happens. From one sunrise to the next takes about one hundred and seventy-six Earth days.
So the line between night and day creeps across the ground. By our rough math, at the equator it moves at about three and a half kilometers an hour on average. That's slower than a brisk walk.
Which means, in theory, you could live on the move. Keep pace with that line, and you'd stay at dawn forever, never facing the full heat of noon or the deepest cold of night.
In Kim Stanley Robinson's novel 2312, a whole city called Terminator does exactly that, crawling around Mercury on rails, just ahead of the rising Sun.

**[CARD: ONE MERCURY YEAR]**

*[year · scene me6_year]*
Stay put instead, and after eighty-eight Earth days, Mercury has gone once around the Sun. Your first year is over.
But your first day isn't. Mercury spins three times for every two trips around the Sun. So one full day, sunrise to sunrise, lasts two whole Mercury years.
Astronomers only worked this out in 1965, using radar. Before that, many thought one side of Mercury always faced the Sun.
And Mercury has a party trick. Its orbit is stretched, so near its closest pass to the Sun, it moves so fast that its orbit briefly outruns its spin.
From some places on the surface, the Sun rises, stops, sinks back below the horizon, then rises again. A double sunrise. And at two spots on the equator, the noon Sun hangs almost still overhead for weeks.

**[CARD: NIGHTFALL]**

*[night · scene me6_night]*
★ Before the Sun goes down, a quick favour. If you're enjoying this, tap like and subscribe. It really helps the channel.
Around eighty-eight Earth days after your sunrise, the Sun finally sets. And the night lasts just as long. Roughly three Earth months of darkness.
With no atmosphere to hold the heat, the ground bleeds its warmth into space, down toward minus one hundred and eighty degrees Celsius.
Your base now needs heaters, not coolers. On Mercury, whether you freeze or fry depends almost entirely on where the Sun is.

**[CARD: ONE MERCURY DAY]**

*[poles · scene me6_poles]*
After one full Mercury day, you'd probably want a better address. And the best one might be at the poles.
Mercury's axis is tilted only about two degrees. So near the poles, the Sun always skims the horizon, and the floors of some deep craters never see sunlight at all.
In 2012, NASA's Messenger confirmed what radar had hinted at. Those dark craters hold water ice. On the planet closest to the Sun.
One estimate put it at a hundred billion to a trillion tonnes. Enough, said lead scientist David Lawrence, to bury an area the size of Washington, D.C. under more than three kilometers of ice.
Much of it is hidden under a dark layer that scientists think is made of organic compounds, probably delivered by comets and asteroids.
For a settler, that ice could mean water to drink, oxygen to breathe and fuel to make. Right next to the endless power of the Sun.

**[CARD: UNDER YOUR FEET]**

*[shrink · scene me6_shrink]*
Now look down. Because the ground beneath you is shrinking.
Mercury is mostly metal. Its iron core reaches out to about eighty-five percent of the planet's radius. Picture a peach that is almost all stone. That's why it's the second densest planet in the solar system, after Earth.
As that core slowly cooled over billions of years, the whole planet contracted. In 2014, scientists using Messenger's maps estimated its radius has shrunk by as much as seven kilometers.
The crust buckled into giant cliffs called lobate scarps. Some run for hundreds of kilometers, and rise up to about three kilometers high.

*[field · scene me6_field]*
That core still makes a magnetic field, but at the surface it's only about one percent as strong as Earth's. A weak shield against the solar wind.
It even twists into what NASA calls magnetic tornadoes, that funnel hot particles from the Sun down to the surface. Your base would need serious radiation shielding.

**[CARD: ONE CENTURY]**

*[einstein · scene me6_einstein]*
Stay a century, and you'd be living on the planet that helped prove Einstein right.
Mercury's stretched orbit slowly swings around the Sun. In 1859, astronomer Urbain Le Verrier found that it swings a little too fast for Newton's laws.
The gap was tiny. Forty-three arcseconds per century. By our rough math, that's about the width of a coin seen from a hundred meters away.
Einstein's new theory of gravity, general relativity, predicted exactly that extra swing. Mercury's orbit became one of its first big wins.

*[twist · scene me6_twist]*
And here's the twist. Mercury is one of the hardest planets to reach. Yet on average, it's the closest planet to Earth.
Venus comes nearest at its closest point. But it also spends long stretches on the far side of the Sun. Mercury never strays far from the Sun, so it never gets that far from us.
In 2019, three engineers ran the numbers in the magazine Physics Today. On average, Mercury is the closest planet to Earth. And to every other planet in the solar system, too. By their math, Mercury averages about 1.04 times the Earth-Sun distance from us. Venus, about 1.14.
So our nearest neighbour, on average, took more than eight years to reach. And starting next spring, BepiColombo will study how this strange little planet formed, and how it changed.

*[outro · scene me6_end]*
So life on Mercury means a black sky, a Sun three times wider, a day that lasts two years, and ice hiding next to the hottest sunlight of any planet.
If you moved there, where would you live? In the frozen craters at the poles, or chasing the sunrise forever? Tell me in the comments.

## What If You Lived in Space for 10 Years?
`b6_orbit` · 1659 words (incl. like/subscribe line) · 10:28

*[hook · scene or6_hook]*
In May, three astronauts flew up to China's Tiangong space station, for what was meant to be a six-month stay.
But China's space agency says one of them won't come home with the others. That astronaut will stay for about a whole year. China's first.
So what if you stayed up there, not for one year, but for ten?

**[CARD: WHAT IF YOU LIVED IN SPACE FOR 10 YEARS?]**

*[news · scene or6_news]*
First, what's actually happening. Shenzhou-23 launched on the twenty-fourth of May, 2026, with commander Zhu Yangzhu, pilot Zhang Zhiyuan, and payload specialist Lai Ka-ying, the first person from Hong Kong to fly to space.
The day before launch, the China Manned Space Agency said one of the three would stay in orbit for about a year, to study what long spaceflight does to the human body.
Which astronaut? That hasn't been announced. The agency says the choice depends on how the mission goes.
Meanwhile, the next ship, Shenzhou-24, was rolled out to its launch pad on the seventeenth of November. It's expected to carry the first foreign astronaut to visit Tiangong, from Pakistan, for about a week. As this video was made, it hadn't launched yet.

*[rules · scene or6_rules]*
Now, the rules of our what-if. You move into a station in low orbit, like Tiangong or the International Space Station. You get food, air, water, and a gym.
You never come down. Not for ten whole years.
And a station like this races around the Earth about every ninety minutes. That's sixteen sunrises and sixteen sunsets every day. By our rough math, ten years is about fifty-eight thousand sunrises.
Every single day, you cover about the distance to the Moon and back. And your body has to deal with all of it.

**[CARD: DAY 1]**

*[fluid · scene or6_fluid]*
The engines cut off, and you float. Within hours, your body notices that something is very wrong.
On Earth, gravity pulls your blood and other fluids down toward your feet. Up here, nothing does. So about two liters of fluid shift upward, out of your legs and into your chest and head.
Your face puffs up. Your legs get skinny. Astronauts have a name for it: puffy face, bird legs. Many say their head feels full, like it's stuffed.

*[sick · scene or6_sick]*
There's also a good chance you feel sick. Roughly sixty to eighty percent of space travelers get space motion sickness.
Your inner ear still expects gravity to tell it which way is down. Your eyes say you're floating. Your brain can't make the two agree.
A typical bout lasts two or three days. Then your brain adapts, and down becomes wherever your feet happen to be.

**[CARD: WEEK 1]**

*[muscle · scene or6_muscle]*
By the end of the first week, your muscles have noticed too.
On Earth, your calves, thighs, back and neck fight gravity every second you stand. Scientists call them antigravity muscles. Up here, they have nothing to fight.
The Canadian Space Agency says astronauts can lose up to twenty percent of their muscle mass on flights lasting just five to eleven days.
That's why astronauts on the space station exercise about two and a half hours a day. In our what-if, so do you. Every day, for ten years.

**[CARD: MONTH 1]**

*[bone · scene or6_bone]*
One month in, a quieter loss is adding up. Your bones.
Bone is alive. It's constantly rebuilt to match the loads you put on it. Take the load away, and your body starts taking bone apart.
NASA says that on missions of four to six months, weight-bearing bones like the spine and hips lose one to one and a half percent of their density every month.
That's fast enough, NASA warns, to put astronauts at risk of fractures and premature osteoporosis, the bone-thinning disease that normally comes with old age.

*[spine · scene or6_spine]*
But you also get a strange bonus. You grow.
Without gravity squashing your spine, the soft discs between your vertebrae relax and spread out. NASA scientists say astronauts can grow up to three percent taller.
For someone 1.8 meters tall, that's about five centimeters.
It isn't all good news. In one study of six astronauts on the space station, more than half reported back pain, and the muscles that support the lower spine shrank by about a fifth.

**[CARD: MONTH 6]**

*[eyes · scene or6_eyes]*
Six months in, the doctors watching you pay close attention to your eyes.
That fluid pushed toward your head never fully drains away. About seventy percent of astronauts on long missions develop signs of something called SANS. Spaceflight associated neuro-ocular syndrome.
The nerve at the back of the eye can swell. The back of the eyeball can flatten. Some astronauts come home needing new glasses.
So far, no astronaut has lost their sight permanently. But nobody fully understands what causes it, and longer missions could make it worse.

*[rad · scene or6_rad]*
★ Quick pause. If this is blowing your mind a little, a like and a subscribe really helps the channel.
Now the danger you can't feel at all. Radiation.
Down here, the atmosphere and Earth's magnetic field shield you. On the ground, people typically get about two to three millisieverts a year.
In low orbit, you're still partly inside that magnetic shield, but the station's walls can't stop everything. Space station crews typically pick up about 0.2 to 0.5 millisieverts every single day.
By our rough math, that's a month or two of ground-level radiation, every day. NASA says one millisievert is about ten chest X-rays. So that's like a few chest X-rays, every day, for as long as you stay.

**[CARD: ONE YEAR]**

*[twins · scene or6_twins]*
One year. This is where the real data gets thin. Very few people have ever done it.
From 2015 to 2016, NASA astronaut Scott Kelly spent three hundred and forty days on the space station, while his identical twin brother Mark stayed on Earth. Ten research teams compared them.
The strangest result? Scott's telomeres, the protective caps on the ends of his chromosomes, got longer in space. Within about two days of landing, they shortened again, and some ended up a bit shorter than before.
His gene activity changed too. About ninety-one percent of those changes went back to normal within six months. But his thinking speed and accuracy dropped after landing, and were still lower six months later.
And remember, it's a study of two people. The researchers themselves warn that you can't draw firm rules from it.

*[records · scene or6_records]*
The record for the longest single stay belongs to Russian doctor and cosmonaut Valeri Polyakov. Four hundred and thirty-seven days aboard the Mir station, from January 1994 to March 1995.
His mission was meant to test whether a human body could survive a trip as long as a journey to Mars. After landing, cosmonauts were usually carried from the capsule. He chose to walk.
The record for the most total time in space belongs to Oleg Kononenko. In June 2024, he became the first person to pass one thousand days. He finished with one thousand one hundred and eleven, over five flights.
Now put our what-if on the same chart. Ten years is about three thousand six hundred and fifty days. More than three times anyone has ever spent up there, even added together.

**[CARD: TEN YEARS]**

*[ten · scene or6_ten]*
So, ten years. Nobody has done it, so from here on we're extrapolating. Treat this as rough math, not a forecast.
Start with radiation. NASA's limit for an astronaut's entire career is six hundred millisieverts.
At 0.2 to 0.5 millisieverts a day, you'd reach that limit somewhere between about three and eight years in. After ten years, you'd have roughly seven hundred and thirty to eighteen hundred. Up to three times the career limit.
Then bone. Nobody knows if bone keeps disappearing at the same pace, because nobody has stayed long enough to find out. NASA itself says more research is needed on longer missions.
And your eyes, your arteries and your immune system are all on the list of things that changed in the twin study. For ten years, the honest answer is: we don't know.

*[mars · scene or6_mars]*
And that's exactly why space agencies care. Because a trip to Mars is the real long haul.
In one NASA reference plan, the flight out takes about one hundred and eighty days, then you stay about five hundred days on the surface, and fly another one hundred and eighty days home. Nearly two and a half years.
On its way to Mars, NASA's Curiosity rover carried a radiation detector. It measured about 1.8 millisieverts a day in deep space. By that measure, just the flights there and back would add up to about six hundred and sixty millisieverts. Already past the career limit, before anyone sets foot on Mars.
That's what a year on Tiangong is really for. Every month someone spends up there teaches us how to keep a body working far from home.

*[twist · scene or6_twist]*
And here's the twist. The ten years in orbit might not be the hardest part. Coming home is.
Remember those extra five centimeters? You lose them. Within a few months back on Earth, astronauts shrink back to their normal height.
A lot of the other changes fade too. In the twin study, Scott Kelly's gut bacteria went back to how they were before the flight, and most of his gene changes went back to normal.
But not everything. In a study of seventeen astronauts, the weight-bearing bones of most of them had only partly recovered a year after landing. The lasting loss was about the same as a decade of normal aging.
And in that study of six astronauts, the risk of a slipped disc was about four times higher in the months after they got home. Your spine stretched out in space, and gravity squeezes it back.

*[outro · scene or6_end]*
So ten years in space would stretch you, puff you up, thin your bones and soak you in radiation. Much of it would heal. Not all of it.
And right now, somebody up there is starting the year that helps us find out how far a body can go. Would you sign up for the ten-year trip? Tell me in the comments.
