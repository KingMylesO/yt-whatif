## What If Earth Had Two Moons?
`b7_moons` · 1476 words (incl. like/subscribe line) · 9:07

*[hook · scene mn7_hook]*
Right now, as you watch this, Earth has two moons.
One is the Moon you know. The other is a tiny rock, caught by Earth's gravity this spring, and it's looping around us as we speak.
So what if that second moon wasn't tiny? What if Earth had two big moons?

**[CARD: WHAT IF EARTH HAD TWO MOONS?]**

*[news · scene mn7_news]*
First, what actually happened. On August 20, Hungarian astronomer Krisztián Sárneczky spotted a faint, moving dot. At first, it wasn't even clear if it was a rock or a piece of space junk.
Follow-up observations settled it. On October 29, the International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center announced a natural object, most likely a tiny asteroid. Its name is 2026 QQ8.
Tracing its path backwards, astronomers found it had been caught by Earth's gravity back in April. Its path isn't a neat circle. It makes messy loops around us, and never comes closer than about five hundred and sixty-eight thousand kilometers. That's about one and a half times the distance to the Moon.
What's still unknown? Its exact size. Estimates range from about one to two meters across. And its exact exit date. It's expected to stay bound through December, then drift back into its own orbit around the Sun, probably early next year.

*[mini · scene mn7_mini]*
And here's the strange part. This happens all the time.
Small rocks that circle the Sun on paths very like Earth's sometimes drift slowly past us, and get caught for a while. One estimate suggests Earth could have as many as six mini-moons at any given time. Most are just too small to spot.
We've caught a few. 2020 CD3 looped around us for months before it left. And in 2024, a rock called 2024 PT5, about ten meters long, stayed for fifty-seven days.
PT5 never completed a full loop. But its colours matched samples of Moon rock. It may be a boulder blasted off the Moon long ago, briefly coming home.

**[CARD: THE RULES]**

*[rules · scene mn7_rules]*
So let's upgrade. Imagine Earth's second moon isn't a boulder. It's a twin of the Moon. Same size, same grey rock, same mass.
We'll put it on a circular orbit at half the Moon's distance, about one hundred and ninety-two thousand kilometers away. We won't worry about how it got there. We'll just watch what happens.
Here's the first surprise. A closer moon has to move faster to stay in orbit. By our rough math, using Kepler's laws, the twin circles Earth in under ten days. That's almost three times faster than the Moon.
And it's close. Light from the twin would reach your eyes in about two thirds of a second. Moonlight takes about one and a quarter.

**[CARD: THE FIRST NIGHT]**

*[sky · scene mn7_sky]*
On your first night, the twin rises in the east. And it's huge.
At half the distance, it looks twice as wide as the Moon. That's four times the area. So when it's full, it should light the ground about four times as brightly as a full moon.
The two moons run at different speeds, so their phases drift in and out of step. Some nights, you get a full twin and a thin crescent Moon. Other nights, both are full, and the whole landscape glows silver. Truly dark nights become rare.
Like our Moon, the twin would soon be tidally locked, always showing Earth the same face. And its phases would come round every ten days or so, from new to full and back again.
And when both moons are up, everything on the ground casts two shadows, each pointing a different way.

**[CARD: THE FIRST TIDE]**

*[tide · scene mn7_tide]*
By morning, you'd notice the sea.
Tides come from the difference in a moon's pull from one side of Earth to the other. And that difference fades fast with distance. It follows the cube of the distance. Halve the distance, and the tidal pull gets eight times stronger.
So the twin pulls on our oceans about eight times harder than the Moon does. Add the Moon and the Sun on top, and by our rough math, the strongest tides would pull more than six times harder than today's biggest spring tides.
How high would the water climb? Real tides depend on the shape of every coast and sea floor, so nobody can say exactly. But low, flat coastlines would flood and drain on a grand scale, every single day.
And there'd be two tidal rhythms, one from each moon, beating against each other. On some days they'd stack up. On others, they'd partly cancel out.

**[CARD: THE FIRST ECLIPSE]**

*[eclipse · scene mn7_eclipse]*
★ Halfway through our new life with two moons, a quick favour. If you're enjoying this, tap like and subscribe. It really helps the channel.
Then, one afternoon, the twin slides in front of the Sun.
Today, the Moon and the Sun look almost exactly the same size in our sky. That's why a total eclipse only just covers the Sun, and lets its glowing outer atmosphere, the corona, shine out around the edge.
The twin looks about twice as wide as the Sun. So it covers it with room to spare. Its shadow on the ground would be far wider, but it would also hide the brightest, inner part of the corona.
And because the twin laps Earth every ten days or so, eclipses would probably come far more often. Sometimes, the twin would even pass in front of the Moon itself. A moon eclipsing a moon.

**[CARD: A MILLION YEARS]**

*[spin · scene mn7_spin]*
Now let's fast-forward. Because tides don't just move water. They act like a brake.
Earth spins faster than the Moon orbits, so it drags the tidal bulges slightly ahead. Friction slows Earth's spin, and the bulge's pull nudges the Moon outward. Today, the Moon drifts away by about 3.8 centimeters a year.
Close in, that braking gets much stronger. Plugging the twin into the standard tidal formula, it would brake Earth's spin about sixty-four times harder than the Moon does.
By our rough math, days would stretch by about twenty-five minutes every million years. With only our Moon, that takes more than sixty million years.
And the twin itself would spiral outward about forty-five times faster than the Moon. Close to two meters a year. In effect, it steals Earth's spin, and uses it to climb away.

**[CARD: THE COLLISION COURSE]**

*[chaos · scene mn7_chaos]*
And that's where the trouble starts.
Two big moons don't ignore each other. Every time the twin laps the Moon, the two pass within about two hundred thousand kilometers, and tug on each other. At that range, the Moon pulls on the twin with roughly one eightieth of the force Earth does.
Each tug is small. But they repeat, again and again. And as the twin creeps outward, the gap between their orbits shrinks. Sooner or later, the tugs start to add up instead of cancelling out.
In 1999, planetary scientist Robin Canup and her colleagues simulated young Earths with more than one moon. Their verdict: systems like this are typically unstable. In most of their runs, it ended one of two ways. The moons crashed into each other, or the inner moon fell into Earth.
We can watch a slow version of this at Mars. Its inner moon, Phobos, creeps about two meters closer to Mars every century. In thirty to fifty million years, Mars is expected to pull it apart.
Exactly when our twin would meet its end depends on details we've made up. But two big moons around one planet is probably not a forever arrangement.

*[twist · scene mn7_twist]*
And here's the twist. Earth may have tried this already.
In 2011, planetary scientists Martin Jutzi and Erik Asphaug proposed that, soon after the Moon formed, Earth had a second moon. About a third as wide as the Moon, and around one thirtieth of its mass.
In their model, it sat at a stable point in the Moon's own orbit, sharing its path, for tens of millions of years.
Then it drifted out of place, and caught up with the Moon. Not with a bang, but at a crawl. About seven thousand kilometers an hour. For two moons, that's about as gentle as a collision gets.
Instead of blasting a crater, it splattered across one side of the Moon, as a layer of rock tens of kilometers thick. And that might explain why the Moon's far side is so rugged and mountainous, and so different from the side we see.
When the dust settled, Asphaug said, you had one moon that might have looked a lot like ours today.
It's still a hypothesis. Other scientists think tides and heat can explain the difference. But the Moon might carry the remains of its twin on the side we never see.

*[outro · scene mn7_end]*
So a second big moon would bring brighter nights, double shadows, giant tides and longer days. Until, one day, the two moons meet.
For now, our tiny second moon is about to leave us. If you could keep a second moon forever, would you want one? Tell me in the comments.

## What If Every Volcano Erupted at Once?
`b7_volcano` · 1525 words (incl. like/subscribe line) · 10:17

*[hook · scene vo7_hook]*
On October 27, 2026, an eruption broke through the sea, just off a small Japanese island called Iwo Jima.
Within three weeks, the island had grown. About fifty hectares of brand-new land. That's roughly ninety football fields.
One volcano did that. So what if every volcano on Earth erupted at once?

**[CARD: WHAT IF EVERY VOLCANO ERUPTED AT ONCE?]**

*[news · scene vo7_news]*
First, what actually happened. Iwo Jima sits about 1,200 kilometers south of Tokyo, and the whole island is the top of an active volcano.
In late October, seismic sensors picked up a sharp jump in activity. On October 27, Japan's Meteorological Agency reported the first photos of an eruption, just off the island's southeast coast.
NASA's Landsat 9 satellite had photographed that coast the day before. When the clouds finally cleared on November 11, it saw new land, still hot enough to glow in infrared, and a long streak of floating pumice.
On November 16, Japan's Coast Guard confirmed it. The new land is joined to the island's southeast beach. NASA estimates it makes Iwo Jima about 1.7 percent bigger.
What's still unknown is how long it will last. Land made mostly of ash is loose, and waves chew it up. After an eruption here in 2023, the new land was gone within weeks.
NASA's Daniel Slayback expects this one to wash away within a few months too. But, he adds, we'll have to wait and see.

**[CARD: THE RULES]**

*[rules · scene vo7_rules]*
Now, the what-if. The US Geological Survey counts about 1,350 potentially active volcanoes on Earth. And that's not counting the long chains of volcanoes along ridges on the ocean floor.
In a typical year, only about forty to fifty of them erupt. Less than one in ten.
So here are our rules. At the same moment, all 1,350 wake up. Each one erupts in its own style. Some ooze rivers of lava. Some explode.
And for now, we'll ignore whether this is even possible. We'll come back to that at the end.

**[CARD: MINUTE ONE]**

*[ring · scene vo7_ring]*
In the first minute, seen from space, the planet lights up. But not evenly.
The glow traces lines. That's because most volcanoes sit where the giant plates of Earth's crust meet. About 60 percent of active volcanoes are on plate boundaries.
The brightest line of all wraps around the Pacific Ocean. It's the Ring of Fire. It runs for about 40,000 kilometers, and it's made up of more than 450 volcanoes.
Iwo Jima is one of them. So are Mount Fuji, Mount Saint Helens, and the long chain of volcanoes down the Andes.
Out in the Atlantic, Iceland glows too. In Africa, a line of volcanoes runs down the East African Rift. And Hawaii lights up in the middle of the Pacific.
Meanwhile, under the sea, where most of Earth's volcanoes are hidden, the long volcanic ridges on the seafloor join in.

**[CARD: DAY ONE]**

*[ash · scene vo7_ash]*
By the end of the first day, the biggest problem for most of the world isn't lava. It's ash.
Volcanic ash isn't soft, like the ash in a fireplace. It's tiny bits of rock, minerals and volcanic glass. It's hard, it's abrasive, and it doesn't dissolve in water.
And aircraft hate it. In 1982, a British Airways jumbo jet flew into an ash cloud from Galunggung, a volcano in Indonesia. All four engines stopped.
The jet dropped about seven and a half kilometers. Then, in clearer air, the crew got the engines going again, and landed safely in Jakarta.
Today, pilots stay away from ash completely. In April 2010, one Icelandic volcano spread ash across the skies of Europe. Over a hundred thousand flights were cancelled in a single week, and about seven million travelers were stuck.
That was one volcano. With ash drifting downwind of hundreds of volcanoes at once, you can expect most of the world's air traffic to stop.

**[CARD: THE FIRST MONTH]**

*[sulfur · scene vo7_sulfur]*
Ash falls out of the sky within days to weeks. The bigger, longer problem is invisible. It's sulfur.
Big explosive eruptions fire sulfur dioxide gas high into the stratosphere, above the weather. There, it turns into a haze of tiny droplets that reflect sunlight back into space.
In 1991, Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines blasted columns of ash and gas up to about forty kilometers high, along with around seventeen million tonnes of sulfur dioxide.
That haze spread around the Earth in about three weeks. Within a year, it covered the whole globe.
And the height record is even more recent. In January 2022, the underwater Hunga Tonga volcano sent its plume about fifty-eight kilometers up, into the mesosphere. The highest plume in the satellite record.
Now picture hundreds of plumes like that, all at once, all feeding the same haze.

**[CARD: ONE YEAR LATER]**

*[winter · scene vo7_winter]*
★ Before we get to the coldest part, a quick favour. If you're enjoying this, hit like and subscribe. It really helps the channel.
One year in, the sky is pale and milky. Sunsets glow red. And the planet is cooling.
We know this works, because it has happened before, with just one volcano. After Pinatubo, the haze cooled the Northern Hemisphere by up to about half a degree Celsius.
In 1815, Mount Tambora in Indonesia produced the largest volcanic eruption ever recorded.
The next year, 1816, became known as the year without a summer. Snow fell in New England in July. Crops failed across much of North America, Western Europe and parts of Asia.
Now add every volcano on Earth at once. Nobody can put an exact number on how cold it would get. But scientists who have looked at this scenario describe darker skies, a sharp drop in temperature, and plants struggling for light. A volcanic winter, worse than any in recorded history.
The good news is that the haze doesn't last forever. After Pinatubo, the cooling faded after about three years, as the droplets slowly fell out of the stratosphere.

**[CARD: TEN YEARS LATER]**

*[co2 · scene vo7_co2]*
So a few years later, the sky clears. But volcanoes don't only breathe out sulfur. They also breathe out carbon dioxide. So, do we swap a freezer for an oven?
Here's a surprise. You may have heard that volcanoes release more carbon dioxide than humans do. They don't. Not even close.
All the world's volcanoes, on land and under the sea, add up to roughly a third of a billion tonnes a year. Humans emit around forty billion tonnes.
That means we emit more than sixty times as much as every volcano on Earth combined.
Even Pinatubo, at the height of its eruption, only matched humanity's carbon dioxide output for about nine hours.
So a single burst, even a huge one, isn't the real carbon danger. The danger comes when eruptions keep going for a very, very long time. Hold that thought.

**[CARD: THE GIANTS]**

*[super · scene vo7_super]*
Of course, volcanoes aren't all the same size. A few are supervolcanoes, able to blast out more than a thousand cubic kilometers of material in one go.
By our rough math, that's enough to bury all of London under more than half a kilometer of rock.
The most recent super-eruption on Earth happened about 27,000 years ago, at Taupō in New Zealand.
Yellowstone is the famous one. The US Geological Survey puts the yearly chance of another giant eruption there at about one in 730,000. And it says there's no evidence that one is coming.

*[twist · scene vo7_twist]*
But here's the twist. Something like this did happen once. Just not all at once.
About 252 million years ago, in what's now Siberia, the ground cracked open, and kept erupting.
The Siberian Traps released enough lava and magma to cover an area the size of the United States, a kilometer deep.
But it took its time. The big eruptions began about three hundred thousand years before the great extinction. And a third of the magma came in the five hundred thousand years after it.
Scientists think its gases, including enormous amounts of carbon dioxide, helped drive the end-Permian extinction. By one estimate, more than 96 percent of ocean species and about 70 percent of land species disappeared.
So the worst volcanic event in Earth's history wasn't thirteen hundred volcanoes erupting in a single day. It was one region erupting, on and off, for hundreds of thousands of years.

*[why · scene vo7_why]*
So could every volcano really erupt at once? Scientists say no.
Each volcano is fed by its own local supply of magma, deep underground. There's no switch that connects them. Science Focus magazine compared it to every bubble in a fizzy drink reaching the surface at exactly the same moment.
On a normal day, a few dozen volcanoes are erupting somewhere on Earth. In March 2022, the Smithsonian's Global Volcanism Program counted forty-eight.
And that's how volcanoes really shape our planet. Slowly. Eruption by eruption. Island by island.

*[outro · scene vo7_end]*
Which brings us back to Iwo Jima. One eruption, three weeks, and about ninety football fields of brand-new land.
Volcanoes can chill the planet, ground every plane, and end an era. But they also build new ground, right in front of us.
So what do you think? Will Iwo Jima's new beach still be there a year from now? Tell me in the comments.
