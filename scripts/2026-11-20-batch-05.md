## What If the Moon Disappeared?
`b5_moon` · 1551 words (incl. like/subscribe line) · 9:41

*[hook · scene m5_hook]*
For about four and a half billion years, the same grey rock has circled the Earth.
It pulls the oceans up and down. It sets the clock for animals all over the planet. It even helps steady the tilt of the whole world.
So what if, tonight, the Moon just disappeared?

**[TITLE: WHAT IF THE MOON DISAPPEARED?]**

*[rules · scene m5_rules]*
First, the rules. At midnight, the Moon vanishes. No explosion, and no rocks raining down on us. It's simply gone, and so is its gravity.
Everything else stays the same. Earth keeps circling the Sun, and the Sun keeps shining.
And here's what we're losing. The Moon is about three thousand four hundred and seventy-five kilometers wide, roughly a quarter the width of Earth.
It sits about three hundred and eighty-four thousand kilometers away. Its light takes about one and a quarter seconds to reach your eyes.

**[TITLE CARD: MIDNIGHT]**

*[night · scene m5_night]*
If it happens near a new moon, you might not notice at all. On those nights, the Moon is up during the day, lost in the Sun's glare.
But on a full moon night, it's instant. Moonlight is just sunlight bouncing off that grey rock. Switch it off, and the landscape goes dark. Your moon shadow disappears, and far more stars come out.
Here's a surprise. The Moon isn't actually very bright. It reflects only about twelve percent of the sunlight that hits it. It's a dark, dusty grey. It only looks brilliant because the sky behind it is black.

*[wobble · scene m5_wobble]*
Something else changes that nobody could feel. Earth and the Moon don't simply orbit one another. They both circle a shared balance point, about four thousand six hundred and seventy kilometers from Earth's center.
That's inside the Earth, but well off-center. So every month, our whole planet wobbles around it.
At midnight, the wobble stops. Earth carries on around the Sun, alone.

**[TITLE CARD: 6 HOURS LATER]**

*[tides · scene m5_tides]*
By morning, sailors and surfers notice. The tide doesn't come in the way it should.
The Moon's gravity pulls hardest on the side of Earth facing it, and weakest on the far side. That stretches the oceans into two bulges, and as Earth spins through them, most coasts get two high tides a day.
The Sun raises tides too. But its pull on the oceans is less than half as strong as the Moon's. About forty-six percent.
So now the Sun is in charge. Tides don't disappear, but they shrink to less than half of what they were. By our rough math, about a third the height of today's biggest spring tides.
And those spring and neap tides are gone. That monthly rhythm came from the Sun and Moon lining up, then pulling against each other. With one partner gone, the tides lose their monthly beat.

*[fundy · scene m5_fundy]*
In Canada's Bay of Fundy, the sea can rise up to sixteen meters between low and high tide. That's about as tall as a five-story building.
Without the Moon, those famous tides would be a shadow of themselves. Tidal power stations, which run on the rising and falling sea, would lose much of their energy.
And the strip of shore that floods and drains every day, home to crabs, mussels and seaweed, would shrink.

**[TITLE CARD: ONE MONTH LATER]**

*[reef · scene m5_reef]*
Over the next few weeks, the strangest effects show up in nature. Because a huge number of living things keep time by the Moon.
On the Great Barrier Reef, many corals release their eggs and sperm all at once, in the nights after a full moon. It looks like an underwater snowstorm.
In 2007, researchers found a light-sensing gene in corals that responds to the faint blue light of the full moon. Corals have no eyes, but they can sense the Moon.
Without moonlight, scientists think that cue is gone. And when spawning drifts out of sync, fewer eggs get fertilized.

*[crabs · scene m5_crabs]*
On Christmas Island in the Indian Ocean, millions of red crabs march down to the sea every year.
The females release their eggs before dawn, as the high tide turns, timed to the Moon's last quarter. Take away the Moon, and they lose their calendar.

*[beetle · scene m5_beetle]*
In South Africa, one kind of dung beetle keeps its ball rolling in a straight line at night by reading the pattern of polarized moonlight across the sky. In 2003, it became the first animal known to navigate this way.
And in the Arctic, during the long polar night, tiny animals called zooplankton swim up and down to the rhythm of the Moon instead of the Sun. Around each full moon, they sink, about fifty meters down, probably to hide from predators that hunt by moonlight.
Without the Moon, some of these creatures lose a compass, a clock or a hiding cue. Many would likely adapt. Some might not.

**[TITLE CARD: ONE YEAR LATER]**

*[eclipse · scene m5_eclipse]*
★ Quick favour before we go on. If you're enjoying this, tap like and subscribe. It really helps the channel.
A year in, you'd notice what's missing from the calendar. No more eclipses. Not solar, and not lunar.
Total solar eclipses are a cosmic coincidence. The Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon, but also about four hundred times farther away. So from Earth, they look almost exactly the same size.
And those few minutes of darkness have changed science. During an eclipse in 1868, astronomers studying the Sun's glowing edge spotted a yellow line in its light that matched no known element. It was helium. Found on the Sun, years before anyone found it on Earth.
In 1919, Arthur Eddington's team used a total eclipse to photograph stars right next to the Sun, and showed that the Sun's gravity bends their light, as Einstein had predicted.

*[sky · scene m5_sky]*
Astronomers do get one upside. Dark skies, every night of the month, with no full moon washing out faint galaxies.
And the month itself loses its meaning. It began as the time from one new moon to the next, about twenty-nine and a half days. Calendars still built on the Moon's phases, like the Islamic calendar, would have nothing left to count.

**[TITLE CARD: MILLIONS OF YEARS LATER]**

*[tilt · scene m5_tilt]*
Now jump far ahead. The biggest question about a moonless Earth is its tilt.
Earth's axis leans about twenty-three degrees, and that tilt gives you seasons. Today it only rocks gently, by a little more than a degree either way, over about forty thousand years.
In 1993, French astronomer Jacques Laskar and his colleagues ran the numbers without the Moon. Their answer was alarming. Over long spans of time, the tilt could wander chaotically, anywhere from zero to more than eighty-five degrees.
Mars shows what that might look like. It has no big moon, and its tilt is thought to swing chaotically, from below ten degrees to above sixty. Tip a planet far enough, and its poles can face the Sun for months at a time.

*[debate · scene m5_tilt]*
But wait. In 2011, a team including Jason Barnes and Jack Lissauer ran longer, more detailed simulations of a moonless Earth.
They found that over hundreds of millions of years, the tilt might wander by only about ten to twenty degrees. That's a much bigger swing than today, but nowhere near the chaos people feared.
So the Moon helps keep our seasons steady. But a moonless Earth might not have been as wild as we thought.

*[day · scene m5_day]*
One thing would change for certain. The Moon's tides act like a brake on Earth's spin. Every century, they add a tiny bit of length to the day. Records of ancient eclipses suggest about 1.8 milliseconds per century.
That sounds like nothing. But it adds up. A fossil shell from seventy million years ago, from the age of the dinosaurs, recorded days only about twenty-three and a half hours long. Its year had three hundred and seventy-two days.
Without the Moon, the Sun's weaker tides would still brake our spin, but far more gently. Your days would stay close to twenty-four hours for much, much longer.

*[twist · scene m5_drift]*
And here's the twist. The Moon is already leaving.
Apollo astronauts left mirrors on the Moon's surface. Observatories still fire lasers at them, and time the echo, which takes about two and a half seconds to come back.
The answer: the Moon is drifting away by about 3.8 centimeters a year. Roughly as fast as your fingernails grow. It's that same tidal brake. As Earth's spin slows, the Moon is nudged into a wider orbit.
So it will slowly shrink in our sky. NASA lunar scientist Richard Vondrak estimated that Earth's last total solar eclipse will happen about six hundred million years from now.

*[origin · scene m5_origin]*
But it won't escape. In about five billion years, the Sun is expected to swell into a red giant, and scientists expect that to wreck the Earth and Moon long before the Moon could get away.
And it all began just as violently. Scientists think the Moon formed about four and a half billion years ago, when a Mars-sized world, nicknamed Theia, smashed into the young Earth. One simulation suggests the Moon could have taken shape in just a few hours.

*[outro · scene m5_end]*
So the Moon isn't just a light in the sky. It's a tide maker, a clock for corals and crabs, an eclipse machine, and a brake on our spin. And it's leaving us, one fingernail at a time.
If it vanished tonight, what would you miss most? The tides, the eclipses, or the moonlight? Tell me in the comments.

## What If All the Ice on Earth Melted?
`b5_ice` · 1708 words (incl. like/subscribe line) · 11:01

*[hook · scene i5_city]*
Antarctica's ice is so thick that, in places, the ground hidden beneath it lies more than two and a half kilometers below sea level.
And there's a lot of it. Melt every scrap of ice sitting on Earth's land, and the oceans rise by roughly seventy meters. That's taller than a twenty-story building.
So what if it all melted? Every glacier and every ice sheet, all at once. Where would the new coastline be, and what would we find under the ice?

**[TITLE: WHAT IF ALL THE ICE ON EARTH MELTED?]**

*[rules · scene i5_rules]*
Here are the rules. We flip a switch, and every bit of ice sitting on land turns to water at once. The meltwater pours into the sea. Everything else about Earth stays the same.
Most of that ice is in two places. Antarctica's ice sheet covers almost fourteen million square kilometers. That's about the size of the United States and Mexico put together. On average, it's more than two kilometers thick.
Greenland's ice sheet is smaller, about three times the size of Texas, but in places it's more than three kilometers thick. Between them, these two sheets of ice hold more than two thirds of all the fresh water on Earth.
In the real world, melting all of this would take thousands of years. We'll get to that. But first, let's flip the switch.

*[budget · scene i5_budget]*
So how high does the water go? Antarctica alone holds enough ice to raise the oceans by about fifty-eight meters.
Greenland adds about seven and a half meters more.
And every other glacier on the planet, in the Alps, the Himalayas, the Andes and Alaska, adds up to only about a third of a meter. Hundreds of thousands of glaciers, and the water wouldn't even reach your knees.
Add it all up, and you get roughly sixty-six meters. The U.S. Geological Survey gives a round figure of about seventy. Either way, the sea climbs higher than a twenty-story building.

*[seaice · scene i5_seaice]*
Notice what's missing from that list. The ice at the North Pole.
Arctic sea ice floats. Like an ice cube in a glass of water, it already pushes aside its own weight in seawater. So when it melts, the level hardly changes.
Hardly, but not zero. Meltwater is fresh, and slightly less dense than salty seawater, so it takes up a little more room. NASA estimates the effect is only about three percent of what you'd get from the same ice melting on land.
So the Arctic Ocean could lose all of its ice, and the beaches would barely notice. The real danger is the ice sitting on rock.

**[TITLE CARD: DAY 1]**

*[americas · scene i5_map]*
Seventy meters later, the map of the world looks very different. National Geographic mapped this exact scenario, and in the Americas, the damage is enormous.
Almost all of Florida disappears, along with the Gulf Coast and the Atlantic seaboard. Miami is gone completely, and the low-lying parts of New York and Boston go under.
On the West Coast, San Francisco's hills become a cluster of islands, and California's Central Valley turns into a giant bay.
Further south, the Amazon basin becomes an arm of the Atlantic Ocean, and Buenos Aires is swallowed.

*[europe · scene i5_map]*
In Europe, London is lost. So is Venice. The Netherlands, and most of Denmark, slip under the sea.
Across the Mediterranean, the water reaches Alexandria, and even Cairo, which today sits more than a hundred and fifty kilometers inland.

*[asia · scene i5_map]*
In Asia, nearly all of Bangladesh disappears, along with much of India's coast. In China, National Geographic estimated that land now home to around six hundred million people would flood.
That includes Shanghai, a city whose center has already sunk more than three meters since the late 1800s, and which sits behind seawalls typically just five to seven meters high.
Africa, as it turns out, loses less land than most other continents, although its coastlines still shrink.

**[TITLE CARD: YEAR 1]**

*[albedo · scene i5_albedo]*
After the flood comes the heat. Ice is a mirror. Fresh snow can bounce back around ninety percent of the sunlight that hits it.
Open ocean is the opposite. It reflects less than a tenth of the sunlight, and soaks up the rest.
Swap millions of square kilometers of white for dark water and rock, and Earth absorbs far more sunshine. That warms the planet, which melts more ice, which warms it even more. Scientists call this the ice-albedo feedback.
And in reality, a world warm enough to melt all of its ice would already be hot. National Geographic put that world's average temperature at around twenty-seven degrees Celsius. Today, it's about fourteen.

*[currents · scene i5_currents]*
Then there are the oceans. Around Antarctica, cold, salty water sinks to the sea floor, and drives a slow current through the deep ocean.
Fresh meltwater is lighter, so it floats on top, and the sinking slows down. One study found that under high emissions, this deep circulation could slow by about forty percent by 2050.
Pour in an entire ice sheet's worth of fresh water, and that slow conveyor could stall. Nutrients would get stuck in the deep, and less would reach the life near the surface.

*[spin · scene i5_spin]*

Even the length of your day changes. As meltwater flows away from the poles and spreads toward the equator, Earth's mass moves farther from its axis.
It's like a figure skater spinning with her arms out. The spin slows down. Researchers estimate that melting ice and rising seas have been stretching the day by about one point three three milliseconds per century since the year 2000.
Tiny, sure. But if emissions stay high, they think this could outdo the Moon's effect on the length of our day by the end of this century.

**[TITLE CARD: 10,000 YEARS]**

*[rebound · scene i5_rebound]*
Now, fast-forward ten thousand years. Something strange is happening on land. It's rising.
Ice sheets are so heavy that they press the Earth's crust down into the hot, slowly flowing rock beneath it. Take the ice away, and the land springs back. But very, very slowly.
We know, because it's still happening. About ten thousand five hundred years ago, an ice sheet three kilometers thick melted off Scandinavia. Today, the coast of the Gulf of Bothnia is still rising by about eight or nine millimeters a year.
In Finland's Kvarken archipelago, that adds about one square kilometer of new land every year. And the crust there still has about a hundred meters left to climb.
On Sweden's High Coast, the highest shoreline from the end of the Ice Age now sits two hundred and eighty-six meters above today's sea.

*[antarctica · scene i5_antarctica]*
Now imagine that across all of Antarctica. With its twenty-seven million cubic kilometers of ice gone, the land underneath finally sees the sky.
And it isn't one neat continent. Scientists expect East Antarctica to be a single large landmass, and West Antarctica to be a scatter of rugged, mountainous islands.
That's because so much of West Antarctica's rock floor lies below sea level. Its deepest known basin, the Byrd Subglacial Basin, sits more than two and a half kilometers below the waves.
Over in East Antarctica, beneath the Denman Glacier, a canyon plunges more than three and a half kilometers below sea level. It's the deepest point on land anywhere. The shore of the Dead Sea, the lowest dry land you can stand on, is only about four hundred meters down.
Over thousands of years, as the rock rebounds, some of those islands would rise and join up. But for a very long time, the new Antarctica would be part land, and part sea.

**[TITLE CARD: THE REAL CLOCK]**

*[clock · scene i5_clock]*
So, back to reality. How long would it actually take to melt all of this?
Not a day. In 2024, NASA measured the global sea rising about six millimeters in a single year. At that pace, reaching seventy meters would take roughly twelve thousand years.
Melting speeds up as the planet warms, of course. Some scientists think melting it all would take more than five thousand years.
In 2015, a team led by Ricarda Winkelmann modeled what happens if we burn all the fossil fuel we can reach. Antarctica ends up almost ice-free, and for the first thousand years, the sea rises about three meters every century.
And their model suggested West Antarctica could tip into decline with far less: around six hundred to eight hundred billion extra tonnes of carbon.

**[TITLE CARD: BEEN HERE BEFORE]**

*[past · scene i5_past]*
And here's the thing. Earth has done this before.
About three million years ago, in the Pliocene, carbon dioxide was last above four hundred parts per million. The planet was about three to four degrees warmer, Greenland was largely free of ice, and the seas stood fifteen to twenty meters higher. Today, carbon dioxide is above four hundred again.
Go back more than thirty-four million years, and there was little or no ice at the poles at all. West Antarctica was covered in forests.
Then the planet cooled, ice began to build on Antarctica, and over millions of years, the seas settled near the coastlines we know.

*[twist · scene i5_finger]*
But here's the twist. If Greenland melted, the sea right next to Greenland would actually fall.
The ice sheet weighs around three thousand trillion tonnes. That much mass has its own gravity, and it tugs the nearby ocean toward it, piling water up against its coast.
Melt the ice, and that pull fades. The water slumps away. And the land, freed of its weight, rises up. Models from Harvard researchers suggest sea level near Greenland's coast would drop by twenty to fifty meters.
So where does the water go? Far away. More than about two thousand kilometers from Greenland, the sea rises more than the global average.
Antarctica works the same way. Its melt would raise the seas most in places far to the north: across the Pacific, the Indian Ocean and the western Atlantic. Each ice sheet hits the far side of the world hardest.
And this isn't just theory. Satellites measuring the sea surface since the 1990s have matched the pattern.

*[outro · scene i5_end]*
So if all the ice melted, the sea would rise about seventy meters, Antarctica would break into islands, your day would get a little longer, and the water would be lowest right next to the ice that melted.
If the coastline moved seventy meters uphill, where would you move? Tell me in the comments.

## What If All Your Bacteria Suddenly Vanished?
`b5_microbes` · 1563 words (incl. like/subscribe line) · 10:18

*[hook · scene x5_body]*
Right now, about thirty-eight trillion bacteria are living in and on your body.
That's more than the number of cells that are actually you. Together, they weigh about two hundred grams. Roughly the weight of an apple.
So what if, in one instant, every single one of them vanished?

**[TITLE: WHAT IF ALL YOUR BACTERIA SUDDENLY VANISHED?]**

*[rules · scene x5_rules]*
Here are the rules. At exactly midnight, every bacterium in and on your body disappears. Your gut, your skin, your mouth. All of them, gone.
Viruses and fungi stay, just to keep things simple. And nothing else about you changes. Your own cells are completely fine.
To make sure the bacteria can't sneak back in, you move into a sterile room. The air is filtered, and every meal and every glass of water is sterilized first.
Labs have kept animals like this for decades. They're called germ-free animals. So we know a surprising amount about what happens next.

**[TITLE CARD: THE HEADCOUNT]**

*[census · scene x5_count]*
But first, how many bacteria are we actually talking about? For decades, textbooks said they outnumbered your own cells ten to one.
That ratio traces back to a quick, back-of-the-envelope estimate from 1972, by the microbiologist Thomas Luckey. It got repeated for more than forty years.
In 2016, a team led from Israel's Weizmann Institute did a careful recount. Their answer: about thirty-eight trillion bacteria, against about thirty trillion human cells.
So it's closer to one to one. And here's the strange part. About eighty-four percent of your own cells, by count, are red blood cells.

*[where · scene x5_count]*
Almost all of those bacteria live in one place: your colon. The part packed with bacteria holds only about four hundred milliliters. A little more than a can of soda.
Your skin carries around a trillion more. And your mouth, despite its reputation, holds less than one percent as many as your colon.
The two totals are so close that, as the researchers pointed out, a single trip to the toilet may tip the balance back in favor of your own cells.

**[TITLE CARD: MIDNIGHT]**

*[vanish · scene x5_body]*
Midnight. The bacteria vanish. And at first, you don't feel a thing.
You're about two hundred grams lighter, and almost all of that is missing from your colon.
Your skin looks exactly the same. Bacteria are far too small to see. A typical one is about a micrometer across. Line up around a hundred of them, side by side, and they'd just about span the width of a human hair.

*[genes · scene x5_genes]*
But you've also lost a gigantic library. A 2010 study catalogued about three point three million different bacterial genes in the human gut. That's more than a hundred and fifty times as many as in your own genome.
Each of those genes is a tool. Tools for breaking down food, making vitamins, and building chemicals your body can't make on its own. And you just lost all of them.

*[odor · scene x5_body]*
There is one bonus. Body odor isn't really the smell of sweat itself. It's mostly made by skin bacteria, which break down molecules in sweat into pungent sulfur compounds.
Some of them can be smelled at just one part per trillion. So with no bacteria, your armpits go a lot quieter.

**[TITLE CARD: DAY ONE]**

*[fiber · scene x5_gut]*
By the next day, your gut notices. Most of the fiber you eat reaches your colon undigested, because your own body can't break it down.
Normally, bacteria take over there. They ferment that fiber into short-chain fatty acids, with names like acetate, propionate and butyrate.
Butyrate is the key fuel for the cells lining your colon. Most of your cells burn sugar. These ones run on food made by bacteria.

*[starve · scene x5_gut]*
So now, they're running on empty. In a 2011 study, colon cells from germ-free mice were found in an energy-starved state. They even started digesting parts of themselves to survive.
When the researchers gave those cells butyrate, the energy shortage went away.
Meanwhile, the fiber you eat just passes straight through. One small upside: bacterial fermentation also makes gas. So, a lot less of that.

**[TITLE CARD: ONE WEEK]**

*[vitamins · scene x5_vitamins]*
After a week, the missing chemistry starts to matter. Gut bacteria make vitamin K, which your blood needs to clot, and several B vitamins.
In studies where volunteers ate a diet low in vitamin K, they stayed fine for weeks. But when they also took a broad-spectrum antibiotic, the level of a key clotting protein in their blood dropped.
Germ-free rats in the 1950s ran into the same problem. They developed vitamin K deficiency unless it was added to their food. So in your sterile room, the menu now needs extra vitamins.

*[cecum · scene x5_mouse]*
Then comes the strangest change of all. Mice raised germ-free grow an enormous cecum. That's a pouch at the start of the large intestine. In mice, it's where a lot of the fermenting happens.
With no microbes to break things down, mucus, digestive enzymes and water pile up inside it. In some strains of germ-free mice, the cecum can reach up to a fifth of the animal's entire body weight.
Nobody knows whether a human gut would do the same. But if yours did, that pouch would weigh about fourteen kilograms. Roughly a beagle.

**[TITLE CARD: ONE MONTH]**

*[immune · scene x5_immune]*
★ Quick favor before we go on: if you're enjoying this, tap like and subscribe. It really helps the channel.
Over the following weeks, your immune system goes through a slow, quiet change. A big part of it sits along your gut, and it learns by constantly meeting your bacteria.
Animals raised germ-free tend to have underdeveloped immune tissue in the gut, fewer of some kinds of immune cells, and less of a gut antibody called IgA.
Your bacteria also work like bouncers. They take up the space, and eat the food, that a dangerous germ would need. Scientists call this colonization resistance.

*[cdiff · scene x5_immune]*
And we know what happens when the bouncers leave. Antibiotics can wipe out many gut bacteria at once. That's when a germ called C. diff can take over.
One CDC estimate counted more than four hundred and fifty thousand of these infections in the United States in a single year, linked to about twenty-nine thousand deaths.
In your sterile room, there's nothing to catch. But your gut is now like a house with nobody home and the doors unlocked.

*[brain · scene x5_brain]*
Germ-free mice even behave differently. In a 2004 study in Japan, they released far more stress hormones than normal mice when they were briefly restrained.
Yet in a 2011 study in Sweden, germ-free mice were bolder. They explored more, and spent more time in bright, open spaces that mice usually avoid.
Giving the mice bacteria early in life reversed these effects. How much of this applies to people is still an open question.

**[TITLE CARD: YEARS LATER]**

*[history · scene x5_history]*
So could you live like this for years? Animals can. But it took scientists a long time to find out.
In the 1880s, Louis Pasteur suspected that animals might not be able to live without bacteria at all. In the 1890s, two researchers in Berlin raised germ-free guinea pigs for the first time, for just a few days.
By the 1940s, labs were raising whole colonies. Bengt Gustafsson in Sweden bred germ-free rats in steam-sterilized isolators. And at the University of Notre Dame, a lab called LOBUND kept germ-free animals inside steel chambers.
Today, germ-free mice are a standard research tool. With a fortified diet, they do fine. Though in some strains, that giant cecum causes serious trouble as they age.

*[bubble · scene x5_rules]*
For humans, sterile rooms are real. But they're a medical tool, not a lifestyle. Babies born without a working immune system may need to stay in sterile isolation while they wait for a stem cell transplant.
So a germ-free you could probably survive, with extra vitamins and a lot of care. But you'd be living in a bubble, with a big part of your body's machinery switched off.

*[twist · scene x5_out]*
And here's the twist. The moment you open that door, the experiment is over.
A Yale study found that a single person's presence can add around thirty-seven million bacteria to a room's air every hour, much of it stirred up from floor dust.
A single day of meals can carry anywhere from about a million to over a billion live microbes, depending on the diet.
Every breath, every handshake, every bite. You'd start picking up new bacteria right away.

*[recover · scene x5_recover]*
And a gut can rebuild surprisingly fast. In a 2018 study, twelve healthy men took a strong four-day mix of antibiotics. Within about six weeks, their gut communities were close to normal again.
But not completely. Nine species that every one of them carried at the start were still missing in most of them six months later.

*[baby · scene x5_baby]*
And here's the best part. You've already done this once. In 2023, a group of forty-six experts concluded that babies in the womb don't carry live bacteria. You started life essentially sterile.
Then, during and after birth, you were colonized. Babies born vaginally first pick up bacteria that resemble their mother's vaginal microbes. Babies born by C-section pick up more skin bacteria first.
So your thirty-eight trillion passengers aren't a fixed part of you. They're a crowd that moved in on day one, and they've been coming and going ever since.

*[outro · scene x5_end]*
So if every bacterium in your body vanished, you'd be a little lighter, a lot less smelly, and missing a huge chemistry set. And the second you stepped outside, they'd start moving back in.
If you could keep just one of your bacteria's jobs, which one would it be? Tell me in the comments.
